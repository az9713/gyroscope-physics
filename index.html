0. Motivation: why a spinning top resists falling

A child's top, a bicycle wheel held by its axle, and a spacecraft control moment gyroscope all display the same surprise: a sideways torque does not mostly make the spin axis fall in the direction of the torque.

Instead, the spin axis moves at right angles to what naive statics suggests. It precesses. This document explains that behavior without hand-waving.

The spine of the argument is short but demanding.

QuestionMathematical objectWhere answered
What does it mean for a body to rotate?A curve $R(t)$ in the rotation group $SO(3)$§1
How does mass distribution enter?The inertia tensor $\mathbb I$§2
What is the governing equation?Euler's equation $\dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$§3
Why is torque-free motion still nontrivial?Two conserved quadratic surfaces intersect§4
Why does a top precess?Gravity changes the direction of $\mathbf L$ instead of mainly changing its magnitude§5
Scope. This is classical rigid-body mechanics. Quantum spin gyroscopes, ring-laser gyroscopes, and relativistic corrections are not mainline topics. They are mentioned only where they clarify what the rigid-body model captures and misses.

1. Rotations, frames, and angular velocity

We begin by making the word "rotate" precise. Later sections use this structure every time a vector is differentiated in a moving body frame.

Definition 1.1 (rotation matrix). A rotation matrix is a real $3\times 3$ matrix $R$ satisfying $R^T R=I$ and $\det R=1$. The set of all such matrices is denoted $SO(3)$, the special orthogonal group in three dimensions.

Let $\{\mathbf E_1,\mathbf E_2,\mathbf E_3\}$ be a fixed inertial orthonormal basis. Let $\{\mathbf e_1(t),\mathbf e_2(t),\mathbf e_3(t)\}$ be a body-fixed orthonormal basis attached to a rigid body.

The rotation matrix $R(t)$ maps body coordinates to inertial coordinates: if a material vector has body coordinate column $a$, then its inertial coordinate column is $Ra$.

Lemma 1.2 (skew form of angular velocity). If $R(t)\in SO(3)$ is differentiable, then $\widehat{\omega}:=\dot R R^T$ is skew-symmetric. Therefore there is a unique vector $\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb R^3$ such that $\widehat{\omega}\mathbf x=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times \mathbf x$ for every vector $\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^3$.
Since $R^T R=I$, differentiating gives $$ \dot R^T R+R^T\dot R=0. $$ Transpose the matrix $\dot R R^T$: $$ (\dot R R^T)^T=R\dot R^T. $$ From $\dot R^T R=-R^T\dot R$, multiply on the left by $R$ and on the right by $R^T$: $$ R\dot R^T=-\dot R R^T. $$ Hence $$ (\dot R R^T)^T=-\dot R R^T, $$ so $\widehat{\omega}=\dot R R^T$ is skew-symmetric. Every $3\times3$ skew-symmetric matrix has the form $$ \widehat{\omega}= \begin{pmatrix} 0&-\omega_3&\omega_2\\ \omega_3&0&-\omega_1\\ -\omega_2&\omega_1&0 \end{pmatrix}, $$ and direct multiplication shows $\widehat{\omega}\mathbf x=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf x$. The components $\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3$ are unique because the entries of the matrix determine them. QED
Proposition 1.3 (transport theorem). Let $\mathbf a(t)$ be a vector whose body coordinates are $a(t)$ and whose inertial coordinates are $Ra(t)$. Then $$ \left(\frac{d\mathbf a}{dt}\right)_{\rm space} =R\left(\frac{da}{dt}\right)_{\rm body} +\boldsymbol{\omega}\times \mathbf a . $$ If $a(t)$ is constant in the body, then $\dot{\mathbf a}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf a$.
By definition $\mathbf a=Ra$. Differentiate: $$ \dot{\mathbf a}=\dot R a+R\dot a. $$ Insert $I=R^T R$ into the first term: $$ \dot R a=\dot R R^T R a. $$ Since $\widehat{\omega}=\dot R R^T$ and $\mathbf a=Ra$, $$ \dot R a=\widehat{\omega}\mathbf a=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf a. $$ Therefore $$ \dot{\mathbf a}=R\dot a+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf a. $$ This is the desired formula. QED
$$ \boxed{\dot{\mathbf a}_{\rm space}=\left(\dot{\mathbf a}\right)_{\rm body}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf a} $$ The entire gyroscope story is hidden in the second term: body-frame vectors acquire extra apparent change because the frame itself rotates.

2. Inertia tensor, kinetic energy, and angular momentum

With angular velocity defined in §1, we next ask how a particular body's mass distribution responds to that angular velocity.

Definition 2.1 (inertia tensor about a point). For a rigid body with mass density $\rho(\mathbf x)$ measured from a chosen origin $O$, the inertia tensor $\mathbb I$ is the linear map $$ \mathbb I\boldsymbol{\omega}=\int_{\mathcal B}\rho(\mathbf x)\big(|\mathbf x|^2\boldsymbol{\omega}-(\mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega})\mathbf x\big)\,d^3x. $$ Here $\mathbf x$ is the position of a material point relative to $O$, and $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is angular velocity.
Proposition 2.2 (rotational kinetic energy). The kinetic energy $T$ of a rigid body rotating about $O$ is $$ \boxed{T=\frac12\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbb I\boldsymbol{\omega}.} $$
The velocity of the material point $\mathbf x$ is $\mathbf v=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf x$ by Proposition 1.3. Hence $$ T=\frac12\int_{\mathcal B}\rho(\mathbf x)|\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf x|^2\,d^3x. $$ Use the vector identity $$ |\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf x|^2 =|\boldsymbol{\omega}|^2|\mathbf x|^2-(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf x)^2. $$ Then $$ T=\frac12\int_{\mathcal B}\rho(\mathbf x) \left(|\mathbf x|^2|\boldsymbol{\omega}|^2-(\mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega})^2\right)d^3x. $$ Rewrite the scalar integrand as a dot product: $$ |\mathbf x|^2|\boldsymbol{\omega}|^2-(\mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega})^2 =\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\left(|\mathbf x|^2\boldsymbol{\omega}-(\mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega})\mathbf x\right). $$ Pull the constant vector $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ outside the integral: $$ T=\frac12\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot \int_{\mathcal B}\rho(\mathbf x)\left(|\mathbf x|^2\boldsymbol{\omega}-(\mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega})\mathbf x\right)d^3x. $$ The integral is exactly $\mathbb I\boldsymbol{\omega}$ by Definition 2.1, proving the claim. QED
Proposition 2.3 (angular momentum). The angular momentum $\mathbf L$ about $O$ is $$ \boxed{\mathbf L=\mathbb I\boldsymbol{\omega}.} $$
The angular momentum of the body is $$ \mathbf L=\int_{\mathcal B}\rho(\mathbf x)\,\mathbf x\times \mathbf v\,d^3x. $$ For pure rotation, $\mathbf v=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf x$. Thus $$ \mathbf L=\int_{\mathcal B}\rho(\mathbf x)\,\mathbf x\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf x)\,d^3x. $$ Use the triple-product identity $$ \mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=\mathbf b(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-\mathbf c(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b). $$ With $\mathbf a=\mathbf x$, $\mathbf b=\boldsymbol{\omega}$, and $\mathbf c=\mathbf x$, $$ \mathbf x\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf x) =|\mathbf x|^2\boldsymbol{\omega}-(\mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega})\mathbf x. $$ Substituting this into the integral gives precisely Definition 2.1. Therefore $\mathbf L=\mathbb I\boldsymbol{\omega}$. QED
Important distinction. Unless $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ points along a principal axis, $\mathbf L=\mathbb I\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is not parallel to $\boldsymbol{\omega}$. This nonparallelism is a central source of rigid-body phenomena.

2.1 Principal axes

The tensor $\mathbb I$ is symmetric because its matrix in any orthonormal basis has entries $$ I_{ij}=\int \rho(\mathbf x)\left(|\mathbf x|^2\delta_{ij}-x_i x_j\right)d^3x, $$ and $I_{ij}=I_{ji}$. It is positive semidefinite, and positive definite if the mass is not confined to a line through $O$.

Proposition 2.4 (principal-axis form). For a three-dimensional rigid body not confined to a line, there is an orthonormal body basis in which $$ \mathbb I=\operatorname{diag}(I_1,I_2,I_3),\qquad I_i\gt0. $$ The axes of this basis are the principal axes, and $I_1,I_2,I_3$ are the principal moments of inertia.
The inertia tensor is represented by a real symmetric matrix in any orthonormal basis. The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices states that such a matrix has three real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis. Choose the body basis to be this eigenbasis. Then the matrix is diagonal, with diagonal entries $I_1,I_2,I_3$. Positive definiteness gives $I_i\gt0$. QED

3. Euler equations and their variational origin

The previous section gave $\mathbf L=\mathbb I\boldsymbol{\omega}$. Now we differentiate angular momentum carefully while respecting the rotating body frame.

Definition 3.1 (body variables). The body angular velocity is $\boldsymbol{\Omega}=R^T\boldsymbol{\omega}$. The body angular momentum is $\boldsymbol{\Pi}=R^T\mathbf L$. The body torque is $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=R^T\boldsymbol{\tau}$, where $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ is the spatial torque about the chosen origin.
Theorem 3.2 (Euler's equations). In body coordinates, rotational dynamics about a fixed point obeys $$ \boxed{\dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}.} $$ In principal axes, where $\boldsymbol{\Pi}=(I_1\Omega_1,I_2\Omega_2,I_3\Omega_3)$, this becomes $$ \begin{aligned} I_1\dot\Omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\Omega_2\Omega_3&=\Gamma_1,\\ I_2\dot\Omega_2+(I_1-I_3)\Omega_3\Omega_1&=\Gamma_2,\\ I_3\dot\Omega_3+(I_2-I_1)\Omega_1\Omega_2&=\Gamma_3. \end{aligned} $$
Start from Newton's angular-momentum law in inertial coordinates: $$ \dot{\mathbf L}=\boldsymbol{\tau}. $$ Write $\mathbf L=R\boldsymbol{\Pi}$. Differentiate: $$ \dot{\mathbf L}=\dot R\boldsymbol{\Pi}+R\dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}. $$ Multiply by $R^T$: $$ R^T\dot{\mathbf L}=R^T\dot R\boldsymbol{\Pi}+\dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}. $$ The body cross-product matrix is $\widehat{\Omega}=R^T\dot R$. It acts as $\widehat{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Pi}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}$. Also $R^T\dot{\mathbf L}=R^T\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$. Therefore $$ \boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}+\dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}, $$ which gives Euler's vector equation. In principal axes, $\boldsymbol{\Pi}=(I_1\Omega_1,I_2\Omega_2,I_3\Omega_3)$. The first component of $\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}$ is $$ (\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi})_1 =\Omega_2 I_3\Omega_3-\Omega_3 I_2\Omega_2 =(I_3-I_2)\Omega_2\Omega_3. $$ The other two components follow by cyclic permutation. QED

3.1 Variational origin

For a torque-free body, Euler's equations also follow from Hamilton's principle on $SO(3)$. This view matters later because gyroscopes are best understood as constrained geometric systems, not merely as component equations.

Proposition 3.3 (Euler-Poincare equation for the free rigid body). Let the Lagrangian be $$ \ell(\boldsymbol{\Omega})=\frac12\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\mathbb I\boldsymbol{\Omega}. $$ Stationarity of $\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\ell(\boldsymbol{\Omega})\,dt$ under variations vanishing at the endpoints implies $$ \dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}=0,\qquad \boldsymbol{\Pi}=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\boldsymbol{\Omega}}=\mathbb I\boldsymbol{\Omega}. $$
Let $\eta(t)$ be an arbitrary body vector vanishing at $t_0$ and $t_1$. The constrained variation of body angular velocity has the form $$ \delta\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\dot\eta+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\eta. $$ Then $$ \delta\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\ell\,dt =\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\boldsymbol{\Pi}\cdot (\dot\eta+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\eta)\,dt. $$ Integrate the first term by parts: $$ \int \boldsymbol{\Pi}\cdot\dot\eta\,dt =\left[\boldsymbol{\Pi}\cdot\eta\right]_{t_0}^{t_1} -\int \dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\cdot\eta\,dt. $$ The boundary term vanishes because $\eta(t_0)=\eta(t_1)=0$. For the second term, use cyclic invariance of the scalar triple product: $$ \boldsymbol{\Pi}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\eta) =\eta\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Pi}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}) =-\eta\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}). $$ Thus $$ \delta\int\ell\,dt =-\int_{t_0}^{t_1} \left(\dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}\right)\cdot\eta\,dt. $$ Since $\eta(t)$ is arbitrary, the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations gives $$ \dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}=0. $$ QED

4. Torque-free rigid bodies and Poinsot geometry

Set the torque in Euler's equations to zero. The resulting motion is not trivial because $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Pi}$ need not be parallel.

Proposition 4.1 (two invariants). For torque-free motion, $$ \dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}=0, $$ the body-frame quantities $$ |\boldsymbol{\Pi}|^2\quad\text{and}\quad T=\frac12\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Pi} $$ are constant in time.
Differentiate $|\boldsymbol{\Pi}|^2$: $$ \frac{d}{dt}|\boldsymbol{\Pi}|^2=2\boldsymbol{\Pi}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}. $$ Using Euler's equation with zero torque, $$ \dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}=-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}. $$ Therefore $$ \frac{d}{dt}|\boldsymbol{\Pi}|^2 =-2\boldsymbol{\Pi}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi})=0, $$ because $\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}$ is perpendicular to $\boldsymbol{\Pi}$. For energy, use $T=\frac12\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Pi}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Pi}=\mathbb I\boldsymbol{\Omega}$. Since $\mathbb I$ is symmetric and constant in the body, $$ \dot T=\dot{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Pi}. $$ Also $\dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}=\mathbb I\dot{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}$, so $$ \dot T=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}. $$ Substitute $\dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}=-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}$: $$ \dot T=-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi})=0. $$ QED

4.1 Stability of principal-axis rotations

Theorem 4.2 (intermediate-axis instability). Assume $I_1\lt I_2\lt I_3$ and consider torque-free rotation about a principal axis. Rotations about axes $1$ and $3$ are linearly stable, while rotation about axis $2$ is linearly unstable.
First study rotation about axis $1$. Let $$ \Omega_1=\Omega_0,\qquad \Omega_2=\epsilon_2,\qquad \Omega_3=\epsilon_3, $$ where $\epsilon_2,\epsilon_3$ are small. Keep only first-order terms in $\epsilon_2,\epsilon_3$. Euler's equations give $$ I_2\dot\epsilon_2+(I_1-I_3)\epsilon_3\Omega_0=0, $$ and $$ I_3\dot\epsilon_3+(I_2-I_1)\Omega_0\epsilon_2=0. $$ Differentiate the first equation: $$ I_2\ddot\epsilon_2+(I_1-I_3)\Omega_0\dot\epsilon_3=0. $$ Use the second equation to substitute $$ \dot\epsilon_3=-\frac{(I_2-I_1)\Omega_0}{I_3}\epsilon_2. $$ Thus $$ \ddot\epsilon_2+ \frac{(I_3-I_1)(I_2-I_1)}{I_2I_3}\Omega_0^2\epsilon_2=0. $$ The coefficient is positive, so perturbations oscillate. Axis $1$ is linearly stable. For rotation about axis $3$, the same calculation gives $$ \ddot\epsilon_1+ \frac{(I_3-I_1)(I_3-I_2)}{I_1I_2}\Omega_0^2\epsilon_1=0, $$ again oscillatory, so axis $3$ is stable. For rotation about axis $2$, let $\Omega_2=\Omega_0$ and perturb $\Omega_1,\Omega_3$. Linearization gives $$ I_1\dot\epsilon_1+(I_3-I_2)\Omega_0\epsilon_3=0, $$ $$ I_3\dot\epsilon_3+(I_2-I_1)\epsilon_1\Omega_0=0. $$ Differentiate the first equation and substitute the second: $$ \ddot\epsilon_1- \frac{(I_3-I_2)(I_2-I_1)}{I_1I_3}\Omega_0^2\epsilon_1=0. $$ The coefficient after the minus sign is positive. Solutions include exponentials $e^{\lambda t}$ with $$ \lambda=\Omega_0\sqrt{\frac{(I_3-I_2)(I_2-I_1)}{I_1I_3}}, $$ so generic perturbations grow. Axis $2$ is linearly unstable. QED

5. The heavy symmetric top

Torque-free dynamics explains the body's internal rotation. A gyroscope also feels gravity. We now add one torque and keep enough symmetry to solve the problem.

Definition 5.1 (heavy symmetric top). A heavy symmetric top is a rigid body with one fixed point $O$, mass $M$, center of mass at $\ell\mathbf e_3$ in the body, gravitational acceleration $\mathbf g=-g\mathbf E_3$ in space, and principal moments $I_1=I_2=:I$ and $I_3=:J$. The body axis $\mathbf e_3$ is the symmetry axis.
Proposition 5.2 (Lagrangian in Euler angles). For the heavy symmetric top, $$ \boxed{ L=\frac12 I(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta) +\frac12 J(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2 -Mg\ell\cos\theta.} $$
The angular velocity of a body described by Euler angles $(\phi,\theta,\psi)$ decomposes into three instantaneous rotations: $$ \boldsymbol{\omega} =\dot\phi\,\mathbf E_3+\dot\theta\,\mathbf n+\dot\psi\,\mathbf e_3, $$ where $\mathbf n$ is the line of nodes. The component perpendicular to $\mathbf e_3$ has squared magnitude $$ |\boldsymbol{\omega}_\perp|^2=\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta, $$ because the part of $\dot\phi\,\mathbf E_3$ perpendicular to $\mathbf e_3$ has length $\dot\phi\sin\theta$ and is orthogonal to the $\dot\theta$ contribution. The component along $\mathbf e_3$ is $$ \omega_3=\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta, $$ because $\mathbf E_3\cdot\mathbf e_3=\cos\theta$. Since $I_1=I_2=I$ and $I_3=J$, the kinetic energy is $$ T=\frac12 I|\boldsymbol{\omega}_\perp|^2+\frac12J\omega_3^2 =\frac12 I(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta) +\frac12 J(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2. $$ The center of mass is height $\ell\cos\theta$ above the pivot, so the gravitational potential is $V=Mg\ell\cos\theta$. Hence $L=T-V$, proving the formula. QED
Proposition 5.3 (two conserved momenta). The coordinates $\phi$ and $\psi$ are cyclic. Therefore $$ p_\psi=J(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta) $$ and $$ p_\phi=I\dot\phi\sin^2\theta+p_\psi\cos\theta $$ are constants.
The Lagrangian in Proposition 5.2 depends on $\dot\phi,\dot\theta,\dot\psi,\theta$ but not on $\phi$ or $\psi$. By the Euler-Lagrange equation for a coordinate $q$, $$ \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}=0. $$ For $q=\psi$, $\partial L/\partial\psi=0$, so $\partial L/\partial\dot\psi$ is constant: $$ p_\psi=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\psi}=J(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta). $$ For $q=\phi$, $\partial L/\partial\phi=0$, so $\partial L/\partial\dot\phi$ is constant: $$ p_\phi=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\phi} =I\dot\phi\sin^2\theta+J(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)\cos\theta. $$ Substitute $p_\psi=J(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)$ to obtain the stated formula. QED

5.1 Effective potential for nutation

The constants $p_\phi$ and $p_\psi$ reduce the top to a one-dimensional problem in $\theta$. This is the cleanest way to see nutation: the tilt oscillates between turning points of an effective potential.

Proposition 5.4 (effective potential). Holding $p_\phi$ and $p_\psi$ fixed, the energy can be written $$ E=\frac12 I\dot\theta^2+V_{\rm eff}(\theta), $$ where $$ \boxed{ V_{\rm eff}(\theta)= \frac{(p_\phi-p_\psi\cos\theta)^2}{2I\sin^2\theta} +\frac{p_\psi^2}{2J} +Mg\ell\cos\theta.} $$
From Proposition 5.3, $$ p_\phi-p_\psi\cos\theta=I\dot\phi\sin^2\theta. $$ Therefore $$ \dot\phi=\frac{p_\phi-p_\psi\cos\theta}{I\sin^2\theta}. $$ The energy is $$ E=\frac12 I\dot\theta^2+\frac12 I\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta +\frac12 J(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2+Mg\ell\cos\theta. $$ Use $p_\psi=J(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)$ to rewrite the spin term: $$ \frac12 J(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2=\frac{p_\psi^2}{2J}. $$ Use the expression for $\dot\phi$ to rewrite the precession term: $$ \frac12 I\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta =\frac12 I \left(\frac{p_\phi-p_\psi\cos\theta}{I\sin^2\theta}\right)^2 \sin^2\theta =\frac{(p_\phi-p_\psi\cos\theta)^2}{2I\sin^2\theta}. $$ Collecting terms gives $E=\frac12I\dot\theta^2+V_{\rm eff}(\theta)$. QED
The sleeping top. The upright state $\theta=0$ is an exact solution: there the gravitational torque vanishes and $p_\phi=p_\psi$. Expanding $V_{\rm eff}$ about $\theta=0$ shows that the upright state is a local minimum of the effective potential, hence stable, precisely when $$ \omega_s^2\;\ge\;\frac{4IMg\ell}{J^2}. $$ A top spun faster than this threshold "sleeps" upright without visible precession. As friction slowly bleeds off spin, $\omega_s$ eventually crosses the threshold, the upright state destabilizes, and the familiar tilt-and-precess motion appears.

6. Fast gyroscope asymptotics

The exact heavy-top equations are rich. The familiar slow-precession formula appears when spin angular momentum is large and the tilt changes slowly.

Theorem 6.1 (slow regular precession of a fast top). Consider a symmetric top with fixed tilt angle $\theta$ and large spin angular momentum $$ L_s=J\omega_s,\qquad \omega_s=\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta. $$ If $\dot\phi^2$ terms are negligible compared with $\omega_s\dot\phi$, then the precession rate is $$ \boxed{\Omega_p=\dot\phi\approx \frac{Mg\ell}{J\omega_s}.} $$
For a fast symmetric top, the angular momentum is dominated by spin along the symmetry axis: $$ \mathbf L\approx L_s\mathbf e_3,\qquad L_s=J\omega_s. $$ During regular precession at rate $\Omega_p$, the vector $\mathbf e_3$ rotates about the vertical. Its derivative has magnitude $$ |\dot{\mathbf e}_3|=\Omega_p\sin\theta. $$ Therefore $$ |\dot{\mathbf L}|\approx L_s\Omega_p\sin\theta. $$ The gravitational torque about the pivot has magnitude $$ |\boldsymbol{\tau}|=|\mathbf r\times M\mathbf g| =Mg\ell\sin\theta. $$ Newton's law $\dot{\mathbf L}=\boldsymbol{\tau}$ gives equal magnitudes: $$ L_s\Omega_p\sin\theta\approx Mg\ell\sin\theta. $$ For $\sin\theta\ne0$, divide by $\sin\theta$: $$ \Omega_p\approx\frac{Mg\ell}{L_s}=\frac{Mg\ell}{J\omega_s}. $$ This is the slow-precession formula. QED
Limiting check. If $M=0$, $g=0$, or $\ell=0$, the torque vanishes and the formula gives $\Omega_p=0$. If the spin $J\omega_s$ increases, the precession slows. Both limits agree with physical intuition and with $\dot{\mathbf L}=\boldsymbol{\tau}$.

6.1 Exact steady precession: two rates, not one

The estimate in Theorem 6.1 is the limit of an exact statement. Demanding a constant tilt in the full equations of motion yields a quadratic in the precession rate, so steady precession—when it exists at all—comes in exactly two flavors.

Proposition 6.2 (fast and slow precession). A heavy symmetric top precesses steadily at tilt $\theta\in(0,\pi/2)$ and rate $\Omega_p=\dot\phi$ if and only if $$ I\Omega_p^2\cos\theta-J\omega_s\Omega_p+Mg\ell=0 . $$ Real solutions exist iff $J^2\omega_s^2\ge 4IMg\ell\cos\theta$, and then $$ \Omega_p^{\pm}=\frac{J\omega_s\pm\sqrt{J^2\omega_s^2-4IMg\ell\cos\theta}}{2I\cos\theta}. $$ For a fast top the two roots separate cleanly: $$ \Omega_p^{-}\approx\frac{Mg\ell}{J\omega_s}\ \ (\text{slow, gravity-driven}),\qquad \Omega_p^{+}\approx\frac{J\omega_s}{I\cos\theta}\ \ (\text{fast, inertia-driven}). $$
The Euler-Lagrange equation for $\theta$ from the Lagrangian of Proposition 5.2 is $$ I\ddot\theta =I\dot\phi^2\sin\theta\cos\theta -J\omega_s\dot\phi\sin\theta +Mg\ell\sin\theta, $$ where $\omega_s=\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta$ is constant by Proposition 5.3. Steady precession means $\ddot\theta=0$ with $\sin\theta\ne0$; dividing by $\sin\theta$ gives the quadratic. The two roots follow from the quadratic formula, and reality of the square root requires $J^2\omega_s^2\ge4IMg\ell\cos\theta$. For large $\omega_s$, expanding the square root shows the minus root tends to $Mg\ell/(J\omega_s)$, recovering Theorem 6.1, while the plus root tends to $J\omega_s/(I\cos\theta)$—free gyroscopic precession barely perturbed by gravity. QED
Nutation frequency. Linearizing the $\theta$ motion about slow steady precession of a fast top gives small oscillations at $$ \omega_{\rm nut}\approx\frac{J\omega_s}{I}, $$ proportional to the spin. Nutation is therefore fast and small for a fast top, which is why a well-spun gyroscope appears to precess smoothly: its nutation is a rapid, tiny wobble that friction damps almost immediately.

7. Geometric mechanics view

Euler equations are coordinate expressions of a geometric fact: rigid-body motion lives on $SO(3)$, and angular momentum is a momentum map.

Definition 7.1 (Lie algebra of $SO(3)$). The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is the vector space of $3\times3$ skew-symmetric matrices. The hat map $\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mapsto\widehat{\Omega}$ identifies $\mathbb R^3$ with $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ by $\widehat{\Omega}\mathbf x=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf x$.
Proposition 7.2 (body velocity is left-trivialized velocity). If $R(t)\in SO(3)$, then $$ \widehat{\Omega}=R^T\dot R $$ is the body angular velocity matrix, while $$ \widehat{\omega}=\dot R R^T $$ is the spatial angular velocity matrix. They are related by $\boldsymbol{\omega}=R\boldsymbol{\Omega}$.
Both $R^T\dot R$ and $\dot R R^T$ are skew-symmetric by differentiating $R^TR=I$ and $RR^T=I$. To relate them, $$ \dot R R^T=R(R^T\dot R)R^T. $$ Act on a spatial vector $\mathbf x=R\mathbf X$, where $\mathbf X$ is the same vector in body coordinates: $$ \widehat{\omega}\mathbf x =R\widehat{\Omega}R^T R\mathbf X =R(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf X). $$ Rotations preserve cross products, so $$ R(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf X)=(R\boldsymbol{\Omega})\times(R\mathbf X). $$ Since $\mathbf x=R\mathbf X$, the vector producing the same cross product in space is $\boldsymbol{\omega}=R\boldsymbol{\Omega}$. QED
Why this matters. The extra term $\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}$ in Euler's equation is not an arbitrary correction. It is the coadjoint transport term caused by representing a conserved spatial vector in a moving body frame.

8. Practical gyroscopes and model limits

The mathematical model has now explained precession, nutation, and torque-free instability. Real gyroscopes add bearings, damping, finite stiffness, sensors, and control loops.

Proposition 8.1 (dissipation changes energy but not the kinematic structure). If a damping torque $-\mathsf D\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ is added in body coordinates, where $\mathsf D$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, then the rotational energy satisfies $$ \dot T=-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot \mathsf D\boldsymbol{\Omega}\le0 $$ for torque-free damped motion.
The damped Euler equation is $$ \dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi} =-\mathsf D\boldsymbol{\Omega}. $$ As in Proposition 4.1, $$ \dot T=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}. $$ Substitute the damped equation: $$ \dot T= \boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot \left(-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}-\mathsf D\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right). $$ The cross-product term vanishes because $\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi})=0$. Thus $$ \dot T=-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\mathsf D\boldsymbol{\Omega}. $$ If $\mathsf D$ is positive semidefinite, the quadratic form is nonnegative, so $\dot T\le0$. QED

Vector identities used repeatedly. $$ \mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=\mathbf b(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-\mathbf c(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b), $$ $$ \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=\mathbf b\cdot(\mathbf c\times\mathbf a)=\mathbf c\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b), $$ and, for any rotation $R$, $$ R(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)=(R\mathbf a)\times(R\mathbf b). $$

B. Exercises with solutions

ConceptualExercise B.1. Suppose $\mathbb I\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ is not parallel to $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$. Explain why the kinetic energy is still $\frac12\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\mathbb I\boldsymbol{\Omega}$.

Solution

The formula comes from summing $\frac12|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf x|^2$ over mass elements. It does not require $\mathbb I\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ to be parallel to $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$. Nonparallelism means angular momentum is not in the same direction as angular velocity, but the scalar energy is still the quadratic form associated with the symmetric tensor $\mathbb I$.

DerivationalExercise B.2. Derive Euler's equations for a symmetric free top with $I_1=I_2=I$ and $I_3=J$.

Solution

Set $\Gamma_i=0$ in Theorem 3.2 and put $I_1=I_2=I$, $I_3=J$. Then $$ I\dot\Omega_1+(J-I)\Omega_2\Omega_3=0, $$ $$ I\dot\Omega_2+(I-J)\Omega_3\Omega_1=0, $$ and $$ J\dot\Omega_3+(I-I)\Omega_1\Omega_2=0. $$ Thus $\Omega_3$ is constant and $\Omega_1,\Omega_2$ rotate sinusoidally with frequency $|(J-I)\Omega_3|/I$.

ComputationalExercise B.3. For $I_1=1$, $I_2=2$, $I_3=3$, and $\Omega_0=10$, compute the linear growth rate for intermediate-axis instability.

Solution

Use Theorem 4.2: $$ \lambda=\Omega_0\sqrt{\frac{(I_3-I_2)(I_2-I_1)}{I_1I_3}} =10\sqrt{\frac{(3-2)(2-1)}{1\cdot3}} =\frac{10}{\sqrt3}\approx 5.7735. $$

ComputationalExercise B.4. A top has $I=0.002\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, $J=0.0008\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, $M=0.5\ \mathrm{kg}$, $\ell=0.04\ \mathrm{m}$, tilt $\theta=30^\circ$, and spin $\omega_s=300\ \mathrm{rad/s}$. Find both steady precession rates and compare the slow one with the fast-top formula of Theorem 6.1.

Solution

Here $Mg\ell=0.5\cdot9.81\cdot0.04=0.1962\ \mathrm{N\,m}$, $J\omega_s=0.24\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$, and $I\cos\theta=0.001732$. The quadratic of Proposition 6.2, $$ 0.001732\,\Omega_p^2-0.24\,\Omega_p+0.1962=0, $$ has roots $\Omega_p^-\approx0.822\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ and $\Omega_p^+\approx137.7\ \mathrm{rad/s}$. The approximation $Mg\ell/(J\omega_s)=0.8175\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ matches the slow root to within about $0.6\%$.

# Reproduce the growth rate in Exercise B.3.
import math
I1, I2, I3, Omega0 = 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 10.0
lam = Omega0 * math.sqrt(((I3 - I2) * (I2 - I1)) / (I1 * I3))
print(lam)  # 5.773502691896258

# Reproduce the two precession rates in Exercise B.4 (Proposition 6.2).
I, J, M, g, l = 0.002, 0.0008, 0.5, 9.81, 0.04
theta, omega_s = math.radians(30), 300.0
a, b, c = I * math.cos(theta), -J * omega_s, M * g * l
disc = math.sqrt(b * b - 4 * a * c)
print((-b - disc) / (2 * a))  # slow root, ~0.822 rad/s
print((-b + disc) / (2 * a))  # fast root, ~137.7 rad/s
print(c / (J * omega_s))      # fast-top approximation, ~0.8175 rad/s
